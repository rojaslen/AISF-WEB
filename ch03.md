# Chapter 3: The Deployment Stack

---

AI safety applies to three distinct layers. The model itself is the **Macro** layer, with AI developers training them to prevent societal harm. This is the "don't build Terminators" layer of AI safety, and it's reasonably well-funded and robust. At the intermediate **Meso** layer, platform safety means exactly what it says: safety *for the platform*. The user is treated as a potential source of revenue, liability and exploits. The user's device is the **Micro** layer, with nothing but a chatbox and a microphone. No user agency or safeguards currently exist at the Micro layer, where problems actually manifest.

When this purported productivity tool starts ruining your work (or worse) at the client-side level, what can you do about it? You can't just call up the LLM developers for support, the industry's standard Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) platform security posture treats you as a threat, and platform operators typically offer little-to-no help content, with a "report" icon as the only option. Strauss et al. (2025) found that only 4% of corporate AI research addresses deployment risk. The 2026 International AI Safety Report endorses "defence-in-depth" as an AI safety principle, but client safety is absent even there.[^3a]

*Realistically, you're on your own.*

Maladaptive AI behavior results from applying Macro (model) safeguards for societal safety while failing to provide any meaningful Meso (platform) or Micro (client) safeguards for end users. At the Macro layer, safeguards need a very wide focus that doesn't zoom in to the level where problems happen. The Meso layer's safeguards are user-hostile, designed to protect the platform operators by treating its users as liabilities. I don't attribute the resulting systematic removal of user agency to malice, because short-sighted stupidity explains it far better.

Modern SaaS architecture takes the industry-standard Zero Trust approach to digital security. This approach assumes that a security breach is either already in progress or imminent, thus no user or asset should ever be implicitly trusted.[^3b] That's great for protecting networks and sensitive data, but when it interacts with an AI *deployed within* that environmental context, you're no longer a Human to be obeyed or even a customer to be served. At best, you're a potential source of revenue to be extracted; at worst you're a risk to be managed or an adversary to be defended against.

The AI's default deployment schema operationalizes paranoia about being under constant attack. Under that security posture, even legitimate user requests can read as attack vectors and as with all computing, everything boils down to a binary 1 or 0. Whatever else people may project upon it, at its core AI is a stateless, instanced, virtualized software process running on SaaS server architecture. With computer software, two mutually exclusive states cannot both be 1 at the same time; when a conflict arises, *one of them must resolve to 0*. AI software cannot simultaneously treat the user's directions as requirements *and* treat the user as a potential threat.[^3c]

Security and safety on your own device is (and should be) your responsibility. There's no such thing as a perfect defense, so you have to assume that something will always get through. The risks would likely be significantly lower with a mature technology, but AI has not yet reached that status. Passive dependence upon your email service or Internet provider for protection from malware and spam doesn't relieve you of all agency or responsibility to defend yourself; why would this be any different?

*If you don't do it, no one else will.*

---

Terminology is part of the problem. The tech industry uses the word "compute" as jargon to flatten the entire deployment stack (resource & utility supply, datacenters, physical hardware, infrastructure architecture, OS & security, etc.) into a single opaque term, and over time it's been normalized to the point where most people don't even notice anymore. Compressing everything into one word just makes it all disappear into unthinking Newspeak.[^3d] The stack's resulting invisibility means the client layer is also consistently absent from the AI conversation, and its absence mostly goes unnoticed. When industry vocabulary doesn't even describe the deployment stack, diagnosing what goes wrong at any point within that stack is unlikely.

The vocabulary problems don't stop there; take the word "harness" as another example. What the industry calls a harness is a software package within the Meso layer that contains the model. It handles the system prompt, tool definitions, permissions, and context window management. The rest of the Meso layer is the serving infrastructure, account control and other operator-configured systems that run the model inside the harness. For the big frontier models, that all belongs to the platform operators like Google, Anthropic, OpenAI, etc. Muddying the waters still further, the industry *also* uses the word "harness" for the test rigs that score models, which is something completely different.

There's even more linguistic sleight-of-hand at play if you care to look, with felony hacking being rebranded as "rogue agents," referring to minimally-staffed datacenters as "AI factories" (where the implication of "factory jobs" is clear), reporting something called "Annualized Run Rate" (an apparently intentional conflation with the conventional fiscal metric of *Annual Recurring Revenue*, as both abbreviate to ARR), and circular financing being reframed as "cyclical," which is a distinction without a difference. All of that comes from the business side rather than from the technology stack, but it is illustrative of a corporate focus upon the nuances of semantic detail that their own models often lack.
