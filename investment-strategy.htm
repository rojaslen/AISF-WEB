Strategy Adjustment — 2026-07-21 (Red Sea Blockade + Domestic Grid Constraint)

Two new developments, one reinforcing the disruption thesis and one working against the demand thesis. Net effect: hold sizing, do not add on the bullish leg.

Red Sea escalation (bullish for disruption thesis, bearish for equity volatility). On 2026-07-20 the Houthis declared a full naval blockade on Saudi Arabia, targeting the Bab el-Mandeb Strait at the southern mouth of the Red Sea, in retaliation for a Yemen blockade and a strike on Sanaa International Airport (Washington Times; Atlantic Council). This is the route Saudi Arabia uses to move roughly 4 million barrels/day of oil to Yanbu on the Red Sea, its principal alternative to the Hormuz-side Persian Gulf terminals. Crude loadings through Bab el-Mandeb have already fallen 36% in two weeks (The National, 2026-07-20); a full closure is estimated to remove roughly 7% of global oil supply from the market (TWZ). Practical effect: Saudi Arabia’s two main seaborne export corridors, Hormuz and now the Red Sea, are both under active threat at the same time, which removes the “route around it” option that existed when only one chokepoint was contested. Read-through to the basket:

  • Aluminum (AA, NHY): reinforces the existing Hormuz-driven tilt from 2026-07-08; do not treat this as a second, separate reason to size up further. One disruption thesis, two chokepoints.
  • Copper (FCX) / Glass (OC): energy-cost floor reinforced again via tighter global oil/LNG.
  • Broader shipping: the Red Sea also carries general Asia-Europe container trade via Suez. Houthi disruption there raises freight and insurance costs basket-wide (steel, cement, general imports), not only through the energy channel. Modest tailwind for domestic producers (NUE, STLD) relative to import-exposed competitors, and a further reason for headline equity volatility per the existing double-edge caveat.

Ohio/PJM grid constraint (bearish for the primary demand thesis, not just noise). PJM Interconnection, the grid operator covering Ohio and 12 other states, is now short roughly 6.8 GW of required capacity, with data center interconnection requests outpacing new generation build (Utility Dive; WKBN, “Data centers cause Ohio’s energy demand to surpass supply”). PJM hit a preliminary all-time peak demand of 168,158 MW on 2026-07-02 (Electric Choice), and DOE emergency orders have authorized PJM to curtail data center load and waive plant pollution limits to protect residential reliability. Ohio alone hosts 230+ data centers, concentrated in the central part of the state (Columbus Underground). Industry coverage frames this as a pattern expected to recur in other data-center-dense grids (Newsweek grid-stress mapping), not an Ohio-only event.

This cuts two ways and should not be read as pure downside:

  • Data center leg: a real timeline risk. If interconnection queues and generation buildout cannot keep pace, some announced hyperscale campuses slip right, slowing the pace (not the direction) of the steel/copper/aluminum/glass demand pull tied to that leg specifically.
  • Gas power generation leg: reinforcing. A documented capacity shortfall is exactly the condition that accelerates new gas peaker/combined-cycle construction, since gas is the fastest dispatchable capacity to build. This basket already treats gas power generation as a co-equal demand vector to data centers (see Material-to-Data Center Mapping / Gas Power Generation Coverage above); a grid shortfall is a reason for more gas-plant construction, not less.
    • See also peripherally-related fracking approval in Wayne Nat’l Forest; given Ohio’s large reserves of natural gas, this restarted push (stopped previously by injunction) might be a preliminary signal that increased gas extraction for power generation is currently being prioritized by DoE over conservation or carbon-sequestration concerns. Not directly data center related since it began in Trump’s first term. Increased natgas supply is irrelevant to gasoline pricing at the pump so that nonsensical talking point is DOA. What it actually does is increase supply for other uses (e.g. on-prem gas turbines), and anything leased in Wayne now would be at minimum 2-3 years out (more likely 4-5) before any extraction could begin, assuming litigation doesn’t halt it again. Overcommitment caution. Multiple independent developments (Gulf buildout scale, Iran conflict, now the Red Sea, now the grid constraint) have landed in the same window, and most of them individually read as bullish for the basket. Stacking confirmatory narratives is precisely the failure mode the defined-maximum-loss framework exists to guard against: a string of headlines pointing the same direction is not the same thing as a string of independent reasons to increase size. The Ohio/PJM item is useful precisely because it is not confirmatory, it is a genuine domestic counterweight, and its presence is a sign the thesis is being read on its merits rather than on momentum. Hold all current sizing (including the aluminum tilt from 2026-07-08) at present levels; no new capital commitment pending clearer resolution on both the Red Sea situation and PJM’s next capacity auction results.

Strategy Adjustment — 2026-07-21 (II): Gulf/Iran Reconstruction as Primary Conviction

Stated base-case assumption (user, 2026-07-21): the broader hyperscale/AI data center capex cycle underneath the primary basket is currently in a discretionary, momentum-driven phase, “irrational” in the sense of risk #11 (AI capex bubble): corporate buyers moving in unison ahead of proven ROI. That demand pillar is expected to slow substantially once fiscal reality checks catch up with it (see risk #11, NVIDIA as lead indicator). The Persian Gulf leg of the basket is reprioritized as the primary conviction going forward, on the grounds that it rests on a fundamentally different, non-discretionary demand driver.

The discretionary/non-discretionary distinction. Corporate AI data center construction can be paused, slowed, or canceled if capex guidance turns; it is optional spending against an uncertain return. War-damaged civilian and economic infrastructure in the Gulf region is not optional to rebuild: destroyed refineries, ports, power plants, hospitals, and housing get rebuilt regardless of how the AI capex cycle resolves. This makes the reconstruction leg structurally more durable than the AI-buildout leg, even though both currently sit inside the same basket and are both nominally “data center and infrastructure construction” demand.

Iran specifically — decoupling need from deal. The 2026-07-08 update marked the Iran Reconstruction Thesis SUSPENDED because the negotiated $300B fund and MOU-linked financing mechanism collapsed. That remains true: no financing vehicle exists, and the External Provider Field constraints (Gulf states will not supply Iran, Russia has no capacity) are unchanged. But the deal being suspended and the need growing are two different variables. Continued, and reportedly (per user assessment, 2026-07-21) increasingly civilian-infrastructure-directed, US strikes on Iran compound the eventual reconstruction bill independent of whether or when a financing mechanism gets signed. Legal and ethical questions about the conduct of the conflict are set aside here as outside the scope of this document; the materials-demand observation is narrower: more destruction now means a larger eventual rebuild regardless of the politics of who pays or when. Practical read: the mechanism to invest in Iran-specific rebuild (Erdemir, Akcansa, CNBM, Holcim Iran-optionality) remains blocked for entry, unchanged from 2026-07-08, but the eventual scale of that opportunity, when the mechanism does open, is assessed as growing rather than shrinking. Watch, do not buy.

Practical reprioritization within the existing basket. The Gulf-region names already in the document, both the Gulf-listed satellites (Ma’aden, Emirates Steel Arkan, Saudi Cement, Reem Cement, Qatar National Cement) and the OECD-listed names with material Gulf revenue (ArcelorMittal, Holcim Gulf-only, ABB, Schneider Electric, Vertiv), become the highest- conviction slice of the basket under this thesis, ranked ahead of the pure-US-domestic, data-center-only names absent the Gulf angle. This does not change the primary basket’s structural weighting (Steel 50/Copper 25/Aluminum 7.5/Nickel 7.5/Titanium 5/Glass 5) on its own; it is a conviction ranking for where within that structure, and among the optional Gulf/concrete add-ons, new capital should be prioritized once trading begins. Execution blocker unchanged from the Gulf section: confirm IBKR access to Tadawul, ADX, DFM, and QSE before any Gulf-listed satellite can actually be bought; if that access is not available at first entry, the OECD-listed Gulf-revenue names (ArcelorMittal, Holcim, ABB, Schneider, Vertiv, all NYSE/SIX/Euronext) are the accessible proxy in the meantime.

Why Equities, Not Futures

  • Direct futures require prohibitive margin and carry forced-delivery risk
  • Futures-backed ETFs suffer structural roll yield decay (contango): funds sell expiring contracts and buy next month at higher prices, creating drag over time; better for short/medium hedges than long holds
  • Commodity pool ETFs issue Schedule K-1 tax forms — burdensome at retail scale
  • Pure-play equities: fractional shares from $1, zero commission on major platforms, standard 1099-B tax reporting, no contract expiry

Demand Profile

  • HUMAIN Riyadh: 6.6 GW by 2034 (Saudi sovereign-backed)

Water/Cooling Constraint

Evaporative cooling plus seawater fails: mineral scaling, chloride corrosion, and membrane fouling make standard hyperscale cooling towers inoperable on Gulf seawater. Gulf data center builds will use closed-loop direct liquid cooling (DLC), immersion cooling for high-density AI racks, or district chilled water from dedicated desalination plants.

Desalination plants are co-infrastructure with every major Gulf campus, not a separate category. Material implications land directly on existing basket positions:

Build-Phase Mix — Structural vs Fit-Out (preliminary Gulf positioning, 2026-07-23)

Gulf datacenter construction splits into two material phases with different instruments and different risk character. Both are live (see the 2026-07-23 build-status update under Demand Profile). A preliminary Gulf entry should weight the mix deliberately, not default to whichever is easiest to buy.

  • Structural phase (front-loaded per campus; the bulk of the announced 6.6 GW / 5 GW programs still ahead). Concrete, structural steel, aluminum. Instruments are the Gulf-listed producers: Ma’aden (1211.SR), Saudi Cement (3030.SR), Emirates Steel Arkan (ESARKAN.AD), Reem Cement (REEM.AD). Character: sovereign-backed, non-discretionary demand (the Strategy Adjustment II thesis). Access opens with Saudi/UAE permissions (est. late July 2026); whole-share, no fractional, but low share price. This is the cleaner expression of the Gulf conviction.

  • Fit-out / MEP + IT phase (near-term spend on the first campuses now; e.g. Stargate phase-1 MEP being finalised, HUMAIN’s first 100 MW pair heading to Q2 2026 go-live). Switchgear, transformers, busbars (copper), cooling, racks, PDUs, UPS. Instruments are the OECD equipment names: Vertiv (VRT, NYSE), ABB (ABB, NYSE/SIX), Schneider (SU.PA, Euronext); copper via FCX (already held). Access: VRT and ABB are NYSE-listed and fractional, buyable now without Gulf permissions; SU.PA needs Euro market access.

  • Key tension to weigh. The fit-out names are not pure-Gulf plays; they are global datacenter-equipment firms whose Gulf revenue is one slice. Vertiv especially is heavily levered to global (mostly US) hyperscale AI capex, which re-imports risk #11 (AI capex bubble), the exact discretionary-demand risk the Gulf-primary reprioritization is trying to sit apart from. The fit-out leg therefore buys near-term phase timing at the cost of diluting the Gulf non-discretionary thesis and adding AI-capex beta. There is no pure-Gulf fit-out instrument (the MEP/IT layer is universally imported), so this beta is unavoidable if the fit-out phase is played at all.

  • Preliminary-entry guidance. Lead with the structural Gulf-listed names (the conviction); treat the fit-out names as a smaller, optional near-term overlay, sized with eyes open to the risk #11 beta. Do not let the fit-out names’ convenience (accessible now, fractional) overweight them relative to actual conviction. Keep preliminary positions small and consistent with the second-$1,000 reserve discipline and the standout-watch trigger.

Grid Capacity & Water Constraint Watch List (State-Level)

Methodology

Media “chatter” is not a usable signal for this (see risk #11’s same objection to the AI-bubble narrative). The following are checkable, dated data points that distinguish a real grid-vs- data-center-demand mismatch from routine growth reporting, in rough order of how confirmed the signal is:

  1. Realized capacity auction shortfall. A grid operator’s own forward capacity auction clearing short of its reliability requirement (PJM publishes these directly). This is a trailing, already-occurred event, the strongest signal available.
  2. DOE emergency order or utility “critical strain” declaration. Federal authorization to curtail large loads (data centers), or a utility publicly declaring critical grid strain during a peak event. Also trailing/realized.
  3. Large-load interconnection queue size relative to installed capacity. Published by each RTO/ISO (PJM, ERCOT, MISO, etc.); a queue that is several multiples of current installed capacity is a leading indicator, weaker than #1/#2 until it starts converting to withdrawals or multi-year delays.
  4. Interconnection wait times. Years-long queues (tracked nationally by LBNL’s annual “Queued Up” report, emp.lbl.gov/queues) signal supply cannot keep pace with requests even before any single project is refused.
  5. Voltage stability / reliability test failures at data center sites. A more technical, earlier-stage signal that new large loads are already straining local grid quality.
  6. Grid operator’s own caveats on its forecast. If the RTO/ISO itself flags its long-term load forecast as possibly inflated (ERCOT has done this), treat the queue size as upper-bound noise rather than a confirmed number.

Water is a parallel, sometimes more binding, constraint on the same buildout and needs its own checkable signals rather than being folded into the power methodology above:

  1. Water-rights/permitting moratorium. A state water authority refusing new supply certificates for groundwater-dependent development. This is a legal bar, not a market signal, and where it exists it is at least as binding as a capacity auction shortfall.
  2. Municipal usage caps requiring supplemental supply purchase. A weaker, cost-based signal (raises the developer’s cost basis rather than blocking the project outright).
  3. Cooling technology actually specified vs. media-assumed. Whether new builds are specifying evaporative (cheap, water-intensive) or closed-loop/air-cooled (water-efficient, more electricity- and capital-intensive) is a check-able fact per project, not an assumption; see the cooling note below.

Arizona note: power is not the binding constraint there, water is

Arizona is a poor fit for the power-focused tiering above and a good fit for the water methodology instead. Arizona gets 300+ sunny days a year and is well suited to behind-the-meter solar-plus-storage: industry surveys put 56% of developers exploring on-site or co-located generation specifically to bypass the 3-7 year grid interconnection queues that define the PJM and ERCOT problem above (construction itself only takes 18-24 months, so bypassing the queue is the economically obvious move). If Arizona hyperscale projects lean on-site solar rather than direct grid draw, as the user’s stated assumption holds, Arizona should not be read as exposed to the same power-shortfall pattern as Virginia, Ohio, or Texas. Its real constraint is water: see the Phoenix AMA entry above, an already-realized legal bar, not a forecast.

Cooling technology check, against the user’s stated assumption. The user’s assumption is that cost pressure will push toward the cheapest cooling option (evaporative, water-intensive) rather than the closed-loop/water-efficient systems highlighted in media coverage, which would worsen Arizona’s water constraint faster than currently modeled. Current reporting runs the other way for new, headline builds: evaporative cooling is cheaper and roughly 10% more energy- efficient, but the water difference is stark (evaporative can run ~5 million gallons/day at hyperscale vs. ~22,000 gallons/day for a comparable closed-loop campus), and industry coverage describes new builds migrating fast to closed-loop/air-cooled designs specifically because the political cost of visible water consumption now exceeds the cost savings, even where evaporative would be cheaper in absolute terms. Microsoft is piloting a zero-water design in Phoenix specifically in 2026, which is a direct, Arizona-specific data point against the user’s prediction as currently observed. That said, the user’s underlying logic should not be discarded, only relocated: the closed-loop preference documented here is concentrated among flagship hyperscalers facing direct reputational and local-political scrutiny (Al Jazeera and Grist both report active local opposition to Arizona data centers over water). A genuine fiscal-reality-check phase (risk #11) would hit second-tier or less scrutinized developers first, and cost discipline reasserting itself there, away from the water-efficient designs currently favored by the majors, is exactly the kind of shift that would not show up in today’s headline reporting yet. Treat “which cooling technology is actually being permitted” as a per-project fact to check at the point of any Arizona-specific allocation, not an assumption in either direction.
